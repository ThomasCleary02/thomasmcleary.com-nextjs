title: "Netscape, the Browser Wars, and Mozilla"
subtitle: "How an open source release outlived the company that started it"

---
In the mid-1990s Netscape Navigator dominated the browser market. By the late 1990s Microsoft Internet Explorer, shipped with Windows and backed by exclusive deals, had taken most of that share. In 1998 Netscape open-sourced its browser code. The company did not recover commercially, but the Mozilla project that formed around the code outlived Netscape and helped shape modern open source practice.

## Market context

Netscape's IPO helped define the early commercial web. Microsoft entered the market aggressively: exclusive distribution arrangements, Windows integration that favored Internet Explorer, and competitive pressure Netscape could not match alone. By the end of 1998 IE held roughly half the market; by 1999 it approached 80%.

A well-known episode from October 1997 captured the tone of the rivalry: after Internet Explorer 4.0's release party, a large "e" logo appeared on Netscape's lawn with a message from the IE team. The competition was personal as well as technical.

## Opening the source

On January 23, 1998, Netscape announced that Netscape Communicator would be free and that its source would be released. Jamie Zawinski registered mozilla.org the next day. In March 1998 most of the Communicator codebase shipped under an open source license. "Mozilla" came from Netscape Navigator's internal codename.

The bet was that a wider developer community could move faster than Netscape could alone against Microsoft. It was also a recognition that the proprietary browser business was failing under that pressure.

## From Netscape to Mozilla

The Mozilla project launched when the Communicator source became public. Contributors expanded beyond a single next Netscape release into browsers, tools, and related work. Early naming included Mozilla 5.0 for the browser and SeaMonkey for the broader suite of browser, mail, editor, and messaging components.

Development was not a straight line. Work on Netscape 5.0 was canceled in favor of a new codebase. Mozilla 1.0 shipped in 2002 with substantial improvements across the browser and related applications. Netscape continued to use Mozilla code for a time, and Netscape funded much of the early engineering, but governance and identity shifted toward the Mozilla organization and, later, the Mozilla Foundation.

## Why it mattered

Mozilla showed that:

- A corporate open source release can outlive the corporation that funded it.
- Community governance can coexist with commercial sponsorship, at least for a while.
- Open source software can compete in consumer products, not only servers and tools.
- Commitment to web standards offered an alternative to proprietary browser lock-in.

Netscape lost the browser wars. The open source project it started did not. That separation — product failure versus project continuity — is one of the clearer early examples of open source as a durable development model rather than a temporary marketing tactic.
