For much of the mid-20th century IBM was not merely a large computer company; it was the default computing vendor for many businesses and governments. Punch cards, mainframes, and bundled services created a deep dependency that later antitrust scrutiny tried to unwind. The episode is still a useful reference when talking about platform lock-in.

## How dominance looked

IBM's position rested on more than individual products:

- Continuous technical lead in systems that became industry reference points
- Hardware, software, and services sold as a tightly coupled stack
- Switching costs that made leaving the ecosystem expensive

That combination looks familiar in later platform businesses, even when the product categories differ.

## The antitrust case

In 1969 the U.S. government filed a major antitrust suit against IBM. Allegations included monopolization of the computer market and practices that limited competition. The case produced a large documentary record. It was withdrawn in 1982 as "without merit," but the years of scrutiny coincided with a more open field for competitors such as Microsoft and Apple.

Historians caution against treating IBM as a single monolith. James W. Cortada has described it as a set of internal factions rather than one uniform institution — which makes the monopoly story more complicated than a simple villain narrative.

## Why the history still matters

Modern large platforms also compete through ecosystems: proprietary standards, bundled services, and network effects that raise the cost of exit. IBM's case does not map one-to-one onto any current company, but it shows how technical leadership plus integration can become structural power.

Open source and open standards are one recurring counterweight: development that is inspectable, portable across vendors, and harder for a single firm to fully enclose. They are not a complete answer to every market-power problem, but they change the default assumptions about who can read, fix, and redistribute the stack.
